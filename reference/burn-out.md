# Burn out (using AI)

Using AI is going to cost you mental energy you weren't spending
before. The marketing makes it sound like AI saves you effort.
Sometimes it does. Sometimes it just moves the effort somewhere
else, and the new place is harder to see, which means it's harder
to recover from.

This piece is about the part nobody's measuring yet.

---

## 1. what AI burnout actually looks like

It doesn't look like regular work burnout. You're not tired from
doing too much. You're tired from a specific kind of mental load
that didn't exist five years ago:

- **Constant verification.** You finish a Claude session and your
  brain has been quietly cross-checking everything it said for an
  hour. That's exhausting in a way that doing the work yourself
  isn't.
- **Decision fatigue.** Every output is "do I take this, edit
  this, redo this, or scrap it?" Multiplied across a day, the
  small decisions compound.
- **Loss of flow.** Working with AI has a different rhythm than
  working without it. You keep stopping to evaluate. The deep
  focus state most people get from doing the work directly never
  arrives.
- **Identity drag.** Some part of your brain wonders whether the
  work was really yours. Even when the answer is clearly yes, the
  question costs energy.
- **Always-on availability of help.** When you could solve any
  problem in 30 seconds, you stop letting problems sit. The "let
  it marinate" thinking that solves the hard ones doesn't get a
  chance to happen.

The symptoms feel like normal tiredness, but they don't recover
the way normal tiredness does. A weekend off doesn't fully reset
it. That's the tell.

---

## 2. signs you're slipping

You don't notice burnout the way you notice a cold. It creeps.
What to watch for:

- You dread opening Claude even though it would help
- You catch yourself re-running prompts you already have good
  output for
- You're using AI to avoid thinking, not to support thinking
- Your work starts to feel interchangeable with anyone else's
- You're getting irritated at the model for things that aren't
  its fault
- Hours pass and you can't remember what you actually did
- You feel less competent than you did a month ago

If three or more of those land, you're not lazy and you're not
broken. You're early. Most people will be here in 18 months.

---

## 3. what to do about it

The fix isn't a productivity hack. It's permission.

**Use it less, on purpose.** Pick one or two tasks a week to do
without AI. Not to prove anything. To remember what the work
feels like in your own hands.

**Stop chasing the productivity ceiling.** AI keeps moving the
bar for what "a productive day" looks like. You can ignore that.
The right amount of output is the amount you can sustain without
hating your life.

**Don't use it when you're tired.** Tired brains can't verify
output. You'll either approve garbage or burn extra energy
double-checking. Better to walk away and come back.

**Take credit for your work, in your head if nowhere else.** The
collaboration is real. The output is yours. The model is a tool,
not a co-author. The identity drag dissolves when you name it.

**Let problems sit again.** Not every question needs to be
answered in 90 seconds. Some of your best thinking is going to
happen between asking and being given the answer, if you let it.

---

## 4. the system part

Some of this isn't on you. If your employer rolled out AI tools
with a productivity expectation attached, you're not the only one
feeling the squeeze. The cost is real even when the org chart
hasn't caught up to it yet.

A few questions to ask:

- Has my expected output gone up since AI was introduced? By how
  much?
- Am I being measured on AI-assisted volume without my workflow
  being redesigned for it?
- Is anyone tracking whether the people on my team are okay?

If the answers point at trouble, you're not paranoid. You're
early. Write down what you're noticing. The first people to put
words on this stuff are the ones who shape the conversation
later.

---

## 5. where this leaves you

You don't have to use AI every day. You don't have to use it for
everything. You're allowed to like it for some things, hate it
for others, and walk away when you need to.

The people who do this well don't talk about it in productivity
terms. They talk about it the way you'd talk about any tool:
it's useful, it has limits, you put it down when it isn't
working.

The hype-and-doom internet doesn't have room for that take. You
don't owe the internet anything.
